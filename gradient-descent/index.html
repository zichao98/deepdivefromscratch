Gradient Descent from zero to hero

Almost every AI model you have heard of learned by doing one simple thing over and over: take a small step downhill. By the end of this lesson you will understand that step completely — and use it to train a real model yourself.

  • Run gradient descent by hand and choose a sensible learning rate

0Chapter 0

The problem: find the bottom

Goal: understand what "training a model" actually means — and why we can't just look at the answer.

When a model learns, it is trying to make its loss — a single number that measures how wrong it is — as small as possible. The model has knobs called parameters. Every setting of the knobs gives a different loss. Training means finding the knob settings with the lowest loss.

🥾

Think of it like

You are standing on a hillside in thick fog, blindfolded. You want to reach the lowest point of the valley. You can't see the landscape — you can only feel how high you are standing right now, and how the ground tilts under your feet.

Why blindfolded? Because a real model has millions of knobs. Its landscape has millions of dimensions — nobody can draw it or look at it. All the computer can do is check the height where it currently stands. Try it yourself below.

1Chapter 1

Slope: which way is downhill?

Goal: measure the tilt of the ground at any point, and read off which way to go.

The tilt of the ground is called the slope. To measure it, take a tiny step of size \(h\) and see how much the height changes. Slope is rise over run:

\[ \text{slope} \approx \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \]

The smaller the step \(h\), the more accurate the measurement. When \(h\) becomes tiny, this number is called the derivative, written \(f'(x)\). That's all a derivative is: the slope at one point.

Here is the key insight. The slope points uphill. Positive slope means the ground rises to the right, so downhill is to the left. Negative slope means downhill is to the right. So to go down, move opposite to the slope.

Where does f′(x) = x − 3 come from?

  1. \[ f(x) = \tfrac12 (x-3)^2 + 1 \]

    Our valley. The lowest point is at \(x = 3\), where the squared part is zero.

  2. \[ f(x+h) - f(x) = \tfrac12\big[(x-3+h)^2 - (x-3)^2\big] \]

    Compute the rise. The \(+1\) cancels out.

  3. \[ = \tfrac12\big[2h(x-3) + h^2\big] = h(x-3) + \tfrac12 h^2 \]

    Expand \((a+h)^2 = a^2 + 2ah + h^2\) with \(a = x-3\).

  4. \[ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = (x-3) + \tfrac12 h \]

    Divide by the run \(h\). This is exactly the "rise ÷ run" number in the lab.

  5. \[ f'(x) = x - 3 \quad (\text{let } h \to 0) \]

    As \(h\) shrinks, the \(\tfrac12 h\) part vanishes. That leftover is the derivative. You just did calculus.

Check your understanding

You are standing at a point where the slope is \(f'(x) = -2\). Which way should you step to go downhill?

Remember

  • Slope = rise ÷ run for a tiny step. The derivative \(f'(x)\) is the slope at a point.
  • The slope points uphill. To go downhill, move opposite to it.
  • At the bottom of a valley the slope is zero.
2Chapter 2

Take a step: the update rule

Goal: turn "move opposite to the slope" into a formula, and learn to pick the step size.

We now know which way to go. How far? We take a step proportional to the slope, scaled by a number called the learning rate, written \(\eta\) (the Greek letter "eta"):

\[ x_{\text{new}} = x - \eta \cdot f'(x) \]

That single line is gradient descent. Steep ground → big slope → big step. Nearly flat ground near the bottom → tiny slope → tiny step, so we naturally slow down as we arrive. Repeat until the steps become negligible — we say it has converged.

The Goldilocks learning rate

Too small and training crawls. A bit too big and it overshoots, bouncing across the valley. Much too big and every step lands higher than the last — the loss explodes (diverges). For this valley the curvature is 1, so anything above \(\eta = 2\) diverges and \(\eta = 1\) lands on the bottom in a single step.

Check your understanding

Remember

  • Gradient descent: \(x \leftarrow x - \eta f'(x)\). Repeat.
  • Steps shrink automatically as the slope flattens near the bottom.
  • Learning rate too small → slow. Too large → bouncing or exploding loss.
3Chapter 3

Two knobs: the loss landscape

Goal: go from one knob to many, and meet the gradient.

Real models have many parameters. With two knobs, \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) (say "theta one" and "theta two"), the loss becomes a landscape you can draw as a map. Each ring is a contour line — every point on a ring has the same loss, like height lines on a hiking map. The centre is the lowest point.

\[ L(\theta_1, \theta_2) = 0.1\,\theta_1^2 + 2\,\theta_2^2 \]

Notice the rings are long and thin: the valley is gentle in the \(\theta_1\) direction and steep in the \(\theta_2\) direction. Remember this — it will matter a lot.

How do we get a slope with two knobs? Wiggle one knob at a time. The slope when only \(\theta_1\) moves is the partial derivative \(\partial L/\partial\theta_1\). Same for \(\theta_2\). Put the two slopes together and you get the gradient, written \(\nabla L\):

\[ \nabla L = \begin{bmatrix} \partial L/\partial\theta_1 \\ \partial L/\partial\theta_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2\,\theta_1 \\ 4\,\theta_2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Computing the partial derivatives

  1. \[ L = 0.1\,\theta_1^2 + 2\,\theta_2^2 \]

    Start from the loss.

  2. \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_1} = 0.1 \cdot 2\theta_1 + 0 = 0.2\,\theta_1 \]

    Treat \(\theta_2\) as a fixed number, so \(2\theta_2^2\) is a constant with zero slope. The slope of \(\theta^2\) is \(2\theta\) (Chapter 1's result with the valley at 0).

  3. \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_2} = 0 + 2 \cdot 2\theta_2 = 4\,\theta_2 \]

    Now \(\theta_1\) is frozen instead.

  4. \[ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla L \]

    The 1-D update rule, applied to every knob at once. Each knob moves against its own slope.

Check your understanding

At \(\theta = (5, 1)\), the gradient is \((1, 4)\). Which statement is true?

Remember

  • With many knobs, take the slope along each one: these are partial derivatives.
  • Stack them and you get the gradient \(\nabla L\), an arrow pointing uphill.
  • Gradient descent steps along \(-\nabla L\), downhill in every knob at once.

With \(\eta = 0.1\) the path first rushes across the steep direction, then crawls for a long time along the gentle floor of the valley — about 200 steps. Raise \(\eta\) to go faster along the floor, and the steep direction starts overshooting: the zig-zag. Raise it past 0.5 and the steep direction explodes.

⚠️

The core weakness

One learning rate has to serve every direction. The steep direction (curvature 4) needs \(\eta < 0.5\) to stay stable, which forces tiny progress in the gentle direction (curvature 0.2). The ratio of steepest to gentlest curvature, here \(4/0.2 = 20\), is called the condition number. The bigger it is, the more plain gradient descent struggles. The next two chapters fix this.

Check your understanding

Why does gradient descent zig-zag in a long, narrow valley?

Remember

  • Plain gradient descent uses one step size for every direction.
  • In narrow valleys (high condition number) it zig-zags and crawls.
5Chapter 5

Momentum: roll like a ball

Goal: use memory of past steps to cancel the zig-zag and speed along the valley floor.

⚽

Think of it like

Instead of a careful hiker, release a heavy ball. It builds up speed going the same way, and bumps from side to side cancel out. After a few bounces it is rolling fast along the valley floor.

Momentum keeps a running velocity \(v\): a blend of the previous velocity and the new gradient. The knob \(\beta\) (beta, usually 0.9) sets how much memory the ball has.

\[ v \leftarrow \beta\, v + \nabla L(\theta) \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, v \]

Why momentum cancels the zig-zag

  1. \[ v_t = g_t + \beta g_{t-1} + \beta^2 g_{t-2} + \dots \]

    Unroll the update: the velocity is a weighted sum of all past gradients, with older ones fading by \(\beta\) each step.

  2. Across the valley, the gradient flips sign every step (+, −, +, −…). In the sum these cancel, so the side-to-side motion is damped.

  3. \[ g + \beta g + \beta^2 g + \dots = \frac{g}{1-\beta} \]

    Along the valley, the gradient keeps pointing the same way, so it adds up. With \(\beta = 0.9\) that's a 10× boost exactly where plain GD was crawling.

Check your understanding

With \(\beta = 0.9\), roughly how much faster does momentum move along a direction where the gradient stays constant?

Remember

  • Momentum averages recent gradients: oscillations cancel, consistent directions accelerate.
  • \(\beta = 0.9\) is the usual default. Too high and the ball overshoots.
6Chapter 6

Adam: a step size for every knob

Goal: fix the root problem — one learning rate for all directions — by giving each parameter its own.

Adam is the optimizer behind most modern deep learning. It combines momentum with one more idea: divide each knob's step by how large its gradients usually are. Steep knobs get smaller steps; gentle knobs get bigger ones. Every direction ends up moving at roughly the same speed.

The Adam update, one line at a time

  1. \[ m \leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1-\beta_1)\, g \]

    First memory \(m\): an average of recent gradients (momentum). Default \(\beta_1 = 0.9\).

  2. \[ s \leftarrow \beta_2 s + (1-\beta_2)\, g^2 \]

    Second memory \(s\): an average of recent squared gradients — how big this knob's gradients typically are. Default \(\beta_2 = 0.999\).

  3. \[ \hat m = \frac{m}{1-\beta_1^t}, \quad \hat s = \frac{s}{1-\beta_2^t} \]

    Both memories start at zero, so early on they are too small. Dividing by \(1-\beta^t\) (step number \(t\)) corrects this bias.

  4. \[ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \,\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat s} + \epsilon} \]

    Step = momentum ÷ typical gradient size. On step 1, \(\hat m = g\) and \(\sqrt{\hat s} = |g|\), so every knob moves by exactly \(\eta\), no matter how steep it is.

Why Adam hovers near the bottom

Because every step is about \(\eta\) long, Adam keeps taking \(\eta\)-sized steps even very close to the minimum, so it jitters around it. In practice we shrink \(\eta\) over time (a learning-rate schedule) to settle in.

Check your understanding

Remember

  • Adam = momentum + a per-parameter step size (divide by the typical gradient size).
  • Defaults \(\beta_1 = 0.9,\ \beta_2 = 0.999,\ \eta \approx 0.001\) work well for most neural networks.

8Chapter 8 · Hero level

Train a real model

Goal: use gradient descent exactly the way machine learning does — to fit a model to data.

Here are 24 students: hours studied and exam score. We want a line \(\hat y = w\,x + b\) that predicts the score. The knobs are the slope \(w\) and the intercept \(b\). The loss is the mean squared error: the average of (prediction − truth)².

\[ L(w, b) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(w x_i + b - y_i\big)^2 \]

The gradient of the mean squared error

  1. \[ r_i = w x_i + b - y_i \]

    Call each prediction error a residual.

  2. \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac1n \sum 2\, r_i\, x_i \]

    Slope of \(r^2\) is \(2r\); and \(r\) changes by \(x_i\) when \(w\) changes by 1 (chain rule). Points far to the right pull hardest on \(w\).

  3. \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \frac1n \sum 2\, r_i \]

    \(r\) changes by exactly 1 when \(b\) changes by 1.

  4. \[ w \leftarrow w - \eta \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial w}, \quad b \leftarrow b - \eta \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial b} \]

    One pass over the data and one update is called an epoch.

What just happened? With raw hours, \(w\)'s gradient is multiplied by \(x\) (up to 10) while \(b\)'s is not: a long, narrow valley again, just like Chapter 4. The largest safe learning rate is about 0.025, which makes \(b\) crawl for hundreds of epochs.

Standardizing the input — subtracting its mean and dividing by its spread, \(x' = (x-\mu)/\sigma\) — makes both knobs equally steep. The valley becomes round, a big learning rate is safe, and training finishes about 50× faster. This one trick is used in almost every real machine-learning pipeline.

  • Features on different scales create narrow valleys. Standardize your inputs.
9Chapter 9

Cheat sheet & final challenge

Gradient descent

\(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla L\). One step size for all knobs.

Momentum

\(v \leftarrow \beta v + \nabla L,\ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta v\). Use \(\beta = 0.9\).

Adam

Momentum ÷ typical gradient size. Default \(\eta = 10^{-3}\), \(\beta_1 = 0.9\), \(\beta_2 = 0.999\).

Loss exploding?

Lower the learning rate (try ÷3 or ÷10).

Loss falling too slowly?

Standardize inputs, add momentum or switch to Adam, then raise η carefully.

Zig-zagging?

Narrow valley: scale your features, or use momentum / Adam.
