Least Squares from zero to hero

Drawing the "best" straight line through data is the first thing every data scientist learns — and the idea behind it powers far bigger models. You will find that line by eye, then by formula, then with matrices, and finally learn when not to trust it.

~40 minutes Beginner → Advanced 6 interactive labs
Start the lesson

You will be able to

  • Explain residuals and why least squares squares them
  • Compute the best-fit line by hand from means, covariance and variance
  • Write regression with several features as \(y = X\beta\) and solve it
  • Derive the normal equation \(\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y\)
  • Use R² and residual plots to judge whether a fit is trustworthy

You need: the equation of a line, \(y = wx + b\), and averages. Helpful: the Gradient Descent lesson, for Chapter 3.

0Chapter 0

The problem: draw the best line

Goal: see that "best line" needs a precise definition — your eye alone isn't enough.

You run an ice-cream stand. For 18 days you wrote down the afternoon temperature and how many ice creams you sold. Hotter days sell more — but by how much? If tomorrow is 30 °C, how many should you prepare?

A straight line \(\hat y = w\,x + b\) answers both questions: the slope \(w\) is "extra sales per degree", and plugging in 30 gives a prediction. The little hat on \(\hat y\) means "predicted", as opposed to the real \(y\). This is linear regression.

1Chapter 1

Residuals, and why we square them

Goal: build the score that defines "best": the sum of squared errors.

For each day, the vertical gap between the real sales and the line is the residual: \(r_i = y_i - \hat y_i\). Positive means the line under-predicted, negative means it over-predicted.

We can't just add residuals up — positive and negative ones would cancel, and a terrible line could score zero. So we square each one (squares are never negative) and add them. That total is the sum of squared errors (SSE), and the line with the smallest SSE is the least-squares line.

\[ \text{SSE}(w, b) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y_i - (w x_i + b)\big)^2 \]

  • Squaring stops errors cancelling, and punishes big misses heavily — so outliers matter.
2Chapter 2

The best line has a formula

Goal: compute the exact least-squares line from a few averages — no guessing.

Here's the remarkable part: for one feature, the best slope and intercept come straight out of averages. With \(\bar x\) and \(\bar y\) the means of temperature and sales:

\[ w = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2}, \qquad b = \bar y - w\,\bar x \]

The top of the fraction is the covariance (do x and y rise together?), the bottom is the variance of x (how spread out the temperatures are). And the second formula says: the best line always passes through the average point \((\bar x, \bar y)\).

Where the formula comes from

  1. \[ \text{SSE}(w,b) = \sum (y_i - w x_i - b)^2 \]

    We want the \(w, b\) at the bottom of this. At a minimum, the slope in every direction is zero (from the Gradient Descent lesson).

  2. \[ \frac{\partial\,\text{SSE}}{\partial b} = -2\sum (y_i - w x_i - b) = 0 \]

    Set the slope with respect to \(b\) to zero. Dividing by \(-2n\) gives \(\bar y - w\bar x - b = 0\).

  3. \[ b = \bar y - w\,\bar x \]

    So the line passes through the average point. Substitute this back in, so every residual becomes \((y_i - \bar y) - w(x_i - \bar x)\).

  4. \[ \frac{\partial\,\text{SSE}}{\partial w} = -2\sum (x_i - \bar x)\big[(y_i - \bar y) - w(x_i - \bar x)\big] = 0 \]

    Now set the slope with respect to \(w\) to zero.

  5. \[ w = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2} \]

    Expand and solve for \(w\). Done — the exact answer, in one pass over the data.

The loss bowl

Goal: see every possible line at once, and connect the formula to gradient descent.

Every pair \((w, b)\) is a line, and every line has an SSE. Plot SSE for all pairs and you get a landscape — for least squares it is always a smooth bowl with exactly one bottom. The formula from Chapter 2 jumps straight to that bottom; gradient descent walks down to it.

4Chapter 4

More features: the matrix form

Goal: handle many inputs at once by writing regression with matrices.

Your notebook also says which days were weekends. Sales probably jump then. Now each prediction uses two features:

\[ \hat y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{temp} + \beta_2 \cdot \text{weekend} \]

Writing this for 18 days is tedious — so stack them. Put each day's features in a row of a matrix \(X\) (with a leading 1 so \(\beta_0\) acts as the intercept), the coefficients in a column \(\beta\), and all predictions become one multiplication:

\[ \hat{\mathbf y} = X\beta \]

  • Stack features into \(X\) (one row per example, a column of 1s for the intercept); predictions are \(X\beta\).
  • Adding a useful feature can improve the fit dramatically.
5Chapter 5 · Advanced

The normal equation

Goal: derive the one formula that solves least squares for any number of features.

Chapter 2's formula generalises to any number of features as the normal equation. The matrices in Lab 4 are exactly its ingredients.

Deriving \(\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\)

  1. \[ \mathbf r = \mathbf y - X\beta \]

    The vector of all residuals at once.

  2. \[ \text{SSE} = \mathbf r^\top \mathbf r = (\mathbf y - X\beta)^\top(\mathbf y - X\beta) \]

    A row times a column adds up products — here, the squares of the residuals.

  3. \[ \nabla_\beta\, \text{SSE} = -2X^\top(\mathbf y - X\beta) \]

    The gradient. Each row of \(X^\top\) is one feature, so each entry says how that feature's coefficient should change.

  4. \[ X^\top(\mathbf y - X\beta) = 0 \]

    Set the gradient to zero. In words: the residuals are uncorrelated with every feature — nothing left for a feature to explain. Lab 4's "Xᵀr" check shows this is (numerically) zero.

  5. \[ X^\top X\,\beta = X^\top \mathbf y \quad\Rightarrow\quad \beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf y \]

    Rearrange. \(X^\top X\) is small (features × features), so this is cheap to solve even with millions of rows.

🔦

The geometric picture

Think of all possible predictions \(X\beta\) as a flat sheet, and the real data \(\mathbf y\) as a point floating above it. Least squares drops a perpendicular from the point onto the sheet — the shadow directly below. The residual is the straight-down line, which is why it's perpendicular to every feature: that's step 4.

⚠️

When it breaks

If two features carry the same information — say temperature in °C and in °F — \(X^\top X\) can't be inverted (it is singular). Near-duplicates make it unstable. In practice libraries use a numerically safer route (QR decomposition) or add a small penalty (ridge regression).

Check your understanding

You have 1,000,000 rows and 5 features plus an intercept. What size is \(X^\top X\)?

Remember

  • Least squares for any number of features: \(X^\top X\beta = X^\top y\).
  • At the solution, residuals are perpendicular to (uncorrelated with) every feature.
6Chapter 6 · Hero level

Can you trust the fit?

Goal: judge a regression like a pro, with R² and residual plots.

Least squares always gives you a line, even when a line is the wrong tool. Two checks catch most problems.

R² ("R-squared") compares your model to the laziest possible one — always predicting the average \(\bar y\): \(R^2 = 1 - \text{SSE}/\text{SST}\), where SST is the SSE of that lazy model. 1 means perfect, 0 means no better than the average.

A residual plot puts each residual against the prediction. For a trustworthy fit it looks like a shapeless band around zero. Any pattern means the model is missing something.

7Chapter 7

Cheat sheet & final challenge

Least squares

Minimise \(\sum (y_i - \hat y_i)^2\): squared residuals.

One feature

\(w = \frac{\sum(x-\bar x)(y-\bar y)}{\sum (x-\bar x)^2}\), \(b = \bar y - w\bar x\).

Many features

\(\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\). Add a column of 1s for the intercept.

R²

\(1 - \text{SSE}/\text{SST}\). Share of variation explained.

Residual plot

Curve → missing shape. Fan → uneven noise. Lone point → outlier.

Watch out

Outliers and high-leverage points pull the line; duplicate features break \(X^\top X\).
