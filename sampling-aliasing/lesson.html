Sampling & Aliasing from zero to hero

Why do car wheels spin backwards in videos? Why is CD audio recorded at 44,100 samples per second — not 40,000, not 20,000? Both answers come from one idea: when you measure a changing signal only at moments in time, a fast signal can disguise itself as a slow one. Learn to spot the disguise, and how to prevent it.

0Chapter 0

Signals and snapshots

Goal: understand frequency, and what a computer actually stores when it "records" a signal.

A signal is anything that changes over time: air pressure hitting a microphone, the voltage in a wire, the brightness of a pixel from frame to frame. The simplest signal is a smooth wave. Its frequency \(f\) is how many full up-and-down cycles it makes per second, measured in hertz (Hz).

📸

Think of it like

Filming a juggler with a camera that takes a photo every so often. Between photos, you know nothing. A computer can't store "a value for every instant" — it stores snapshots, called samples, taken at a fixed rate.

The sample rate \(f_s\) is how many snapshots per second. Sample number \(n\) is taken at time \(t = n / f_s\).

More samples cost more storage and bandwidth, so engineers want the fewest samples that still capture the signal. How few is too few? The next chapter shows the surprising answer — and the trap hiding just past it.

Remember

  • Frequency \(f\) = cycles per second; sample rate \(f_s\) = snapshots per second.
  • The samples are the only information the digital system ever gets.
1Chapter 1

Aliasing: a fast wave in disguise

Goal: see aliasing happen, and find the Nyquist limit yourself.

Too few samples doesn't only lose detail — it can create a lie. If the wave moves too fast between snapshots, the dots fit a completely different, slower wave perfectly. That impostor is called an alias. Since the computer has only the dots, it has no way to know it was fooled.

Try this

  2. Make a 17 Hz signal look exactly like a 3 Hz one. (There's more than one answer.)
  3. Make a moving wave look perfectly frozen — a flat line of samples.
  4. For a 9 Hz signal, find the smallest whole-number sample rate that doesn't alias.

You found the rule. A wave is captured faithfully only while

\[ f \;<\; \frac{f_s}{2} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad f_s \;>\; 2f \]

The boundary \(f_s/2\) is the Nyquist frequency: the highest frequency a sampler can represent. Intuitively, you need more than two samples per cycle — at least one on the way up and one on the way down. Exactly two (\(f_s = 2f\)) isn't enough: the samples might all land at the same height, and you can't tell the wave's size or even whether it's there.

Remember

  • Nyquist limit = \(f_s/2\). Faster waves get disguised as slower aliases.
  • An alias fits the samples perfectly — after sampling, the damage is invisible and irreversible.

The wagon-wheel effect

Goal: recognise aliasing in something you've seen with your own eyes.

A film camera is a sampler: it takes 24 photos per second. A spinning wheel is a signal: its angle changes over time. If the wheel turns almost a full circle between two frames, the film shows it turning a tiny bit backwards. Your brain picks the shortest explanation for the dots — just like the alias curve in Lab 1.

This is the same maths: a speed of \(r\) revolutions per second filmed at \(f_s\) frames per second looks like \(r - k f_s\) for whichever whole number \(k\) makes it smallest. Helicopter rotors that seem to hover motionless on video, and LED lights that flicker on camera, are all aliasing.

Remember

  • Any repeating motion sampled too slowly aliases: rotation looks slower, frozen or reversed.
3Chapter 3

Frequency folding: predicting the alias

Goal: compute any alias in your head, and see why it happens mathematically.

Picture the frequency axis as a long strip of paper, folded like an accordion at every multiple of \(f_s/2\). Every frequency lands on the first panel, between 0 and \(f_s/2\) — that's where it ends up after sampling. The recipe:

\[ f_{\text{alias}} = \big|\, f - k f_s \,\big| \quad\text{with } k \text{ the whole number that makes it smallest} \]

Try this
  1. Move into the first fold (5–10 Hz). The alias mirrors: f → 10 − f.
  2. Find three different true frequencies that all appear as 2 Hz.

Why does this happen? Because the samples of a sine wave only see the wave at times \(t = n/f_s\), and adding a whole number of full turns changes nothing:

Why 17 Hz and 3 Hz look identical at fs = 20

  1. \[ x[n] = \sin\!\Big(2\pi \cdot 17 \cdot \tfrac{n}{20}\Big) \]

    The samples of a 17 Hz wave: its value at times \(n/20\).

  2. \[ 17 = 20 - 3 \;\Rightarrow\; x[n] = \sin\!\Big(2\pi n - 2\pi \cdot 3 \cdot \tfrac{n}{20}\Big) \]

    Rewrite 17 as 20 − 3 and multiply out. The first term is \(2\pi n\): a whole number of full circles.

  3. \[ \sin(2\pi n - \theta) = \sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta \]

    Adding full circles does nothing to a sine — \(n\) is a whole number, which is the whole point of sampling.

  4. \[ x[n] = -\sin\!\Big(2\pi \cdot 3 \cdot \tfrac{n}{20}\Big) \]

    The samples are exactly those of a (flipped) 3 Hz wave. No algorithm could ever tell them apart. In general \(f\) and \(f \pm k f_s\) are indistinguishable, and \(f\) and \(f_s - f\) differ only by a flip.

Check your understanding

A 7 Hz signal is sampled at 12 Hz. What frequency will it appear to be?

Remember

  • \(f_{\text{alias}} = |f - k f_s|\), folded into \([0, f_s/2]\).
  • Frequencies \(f_s/2\) apart mirror; frequencies \(f_s\) apart are identical.
4Chapter 4

Rebuilding the wave: the sampling theorem

Goal: see that samples above the Nyquist rate lose nothing.

Here's the good news, and it's astonishing. The sampling theorem (Nyquist–Shannon) says: if a signal contains no frequencies at or above \(f_s/2\) — if it's band-limited — then its samples determine it perfectly, at every instant between them too. Not approximately. Exactly.

The catch is how you connect the dots. Holding each value flat (a staircase) or drawing straight lines are crude guesses. The perfect method places a sinc pulse, \(\text{sinc}(u) = \sin(\pi u)/(\pi u)\), on every sample and adds them up:

\[ x(t) = \sum_{n} x[n]\;\text{sinc}\big(f_s t - n\big) \]

  2. With straight lines, push the error below 0.06. How many samples per cycle did it take?
  3. With sinc, get the error below 0.02 using at most 8 samples per second.

Sinc reconstruction rebuilt the 3 Hz wave essentially perfectly from 7 samples per second — just over 2 per cycle — while straight lines needed around 20. Below the Nyquist rate even the perfect method fails, because the samples genuinely describe the alias. The theorem's promise only holds when the signal obeys its condition.

🔍

Fine print

A true sinc pulse stretches forever in both directions, so real systems use shortened (windowed) versions and sample a bit faster than 2f to leave room. That's why CD audio uses 44.1 kHz for 20 kHz of hearing, not exactly 40 kHz.

Remember

  • Band-limited below \(f_s/2\) ⇒ the samples contain everything.
  • Perfect rebuild = sum of sinc pulses, one per sample.
5Chapter 5

Anti-aliasing: filter before you sample

Goal: protect a real recording from aliasing.

Real signals are messy. You want to record a 2 Hz rhythm, but the sensor also picks up a 9 Hz hum. If you sample at 12 Hz, the hum is above the 6 Hz limit — so it aliases to 3 Hz and lands inside your data, looking like a real part of the signal. Once sampled, nothing can remove it.

The fix is an anti-aliasing filter: an analog low-pass filter placed before the sampler that removes everything above \(f_s/2\). The rule: filter, then sample.

Lab 5

Sample rate fs12 Hz
Hum appears at3 Hz

Try this
  1. At fs = 12 with no filter, find where the 9 Hz hum shows up in the spectrum.
  2. Without the filter, choose a sample rate that drops the hum exactly on top of the 2 Hz signal.
  3. Turn on the filter at a sample rate below 18 Hz and get a clean 2 Hz recording.
  4. Filter off: sample fast enough that the hum stays at its true 9 Hz.

At 7 or 11 Hz the hum lands right on the 2 Hz signal, and they merge into one wave. No later processing can separate them. Sampling faster also works, since the hum then stays at 9 Hz where a digital filter can remove it later. That's the everyday trade-off: a sharp analog filter, or a faster sampler (oversampling) with a gentle filter.

Check your understanding

Why must the anti-aliasing filter come before the sampler rather than after?

Remember

  • Filter out everything above \(f_s/2\) before sampling.
  • Aliasing can't be undone afterwards; prevention is the only cure.

Aliasing in the real world

Goal: connect the idea to systems you use every day.

SystemSamplerWhat aliasing looks likeThe defence

Notice the pattern in the right-hand column: every defence is some kind of averaging or blurring before sampling. Motion blur, optical filters and supersampling are all low-pass filters in disguise. In space instead of time, "frequency" means how quickly a pattern repeats across the image; the CNN lesson's stride-2 layers are samplers too.

Remember

  • Anything that measures at intervals — in time or space — can alias.
  • The cure is always the same: remove detail too fine for the sampler, before sampling.
